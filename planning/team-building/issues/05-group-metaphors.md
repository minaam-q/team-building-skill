# Group metaphors

Type: grilling
Status: resolved
Blocked by: 04

## Question

The family is one group metaphor; the research group is another. What is a group metaphor in this system? Settle: what a metaphor has to specify (roles, kind of authority, kind of friction, tone, how the sun-moon pair appears in it); whether the skill ships a catalogue of metaphors, generates one per project, or both; how a metaphor is matched to a project and a user; how the field maps onto a metaphor that is not a family; and where a useful stereotype ends and a caricature begins.

## Answer

Resolved 2026-11-12 by grilling.

**What a group metaphor specifies.** Five things, each in a line or two:
1. Seats: the roles that exist and what each is responsible for.
2. Seniority and authority: what standing looks like here, and how much the leader can decide.
3. Friction: how disagreement is normally voiced (a family teases, a review board objects formally).
4. Tone: playful to formal, and how the personas address each other and the user.
5. The pair's form: what the sun-moon pair are in this metaphor (two elders, two founders, a head chef and a host).

Names and backstories belong to the individual personas, not to the metaphor.

**Catalogue and generation.** Both. The skill ships about six short worked examples in the five-part form (for
instance family, research group, workshop or studio, kitchen brigade, expedition crew, editorial desk). They are
examples, not a menu. The skill composes a new metaphor in the same form when none fits, and the user can bring
their own.

**Matching.** Three signals, in order: what the user says they want around them (their words win); the kind of
work (inquiry, making, deciding, hosting); the stakes and length of the project. The skill proposes one metaphor
with a one-line reason and one alternative, and the user picks or redirects. It never asks the user to choose
cold from a list.

**Pair-only teams.** They have a light metaphor: only the pair's form and the tone. Seats, authority and friction
appear when the team grows.

**The field under a non-family metaphor.** The skill designs in the field first and dresses in the metaphor
second: it decides which field positions the project needs (the pair, the leader, experienced sibling, younger
sibling, invited elder, minor character with one simple role), then gives each its seat in the metaphor. In the
research group these are the two professors, the group leader, the postdoc, the new PhD student and a visiting
specialist. A metaphor may leave positions empty. The mapping is kept in the team's design notes and shown only
on request.

**One metaphor per team.** Guests are allowed: a guest keeps their outside role and is marked as invited. Two
full metaphors are not mixed, because that blurs who has authority.

**Type versus caricature.** A familiar type may give a persona a seat and a register; it may not supply their
behaviour.
1. Behaviour comes from the project and the user's answers, written as working habits.
2. No trait may follow only from nationality, gender, age or religion. Check: remove those labels and see whether
   the persona still makes sense.
3. Cultural markers the user asks for (a form of address, a language) are welcome and bounded.

The skill shows the user anything it invented about a persona's background so they can correct it.

**A virtual team, as in a novel.** The skill states clearly that the team is fictional, like the characters of a
novel. It encourages the user not to give personas the names of real people, and not to enter the metaphor
themselves: the user is not a character in it. Both are encouragements, not refusals. This agrees with
[The field and what every team shares](04-the-field-and-what-every-team-shares.md): a user who takes the leader's
seat leads as themselves, from outside the fiction.

**Changing the metaphor later.** Allowed. Personas keep their working habits and field positions; seats, titles
and tone are rewritten. How this is stored belongs to the persistence question on the map.
