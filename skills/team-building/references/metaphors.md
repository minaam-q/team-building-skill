# Group metaphors

A group metaphor is the surface form a team takes for one project and one user. A team has one.

## The five parts

1. **Seats:** the roles that exist and what each is responsible for.
2. **Seniority and authority:** what standing looks like here, and how much the leader can decide.
3. **Friction:** how disagreement is normally voiced.
4. **Tone:** playful to formal, and how the personas address each other and the user.
5. **The pair's form:** what the two seniors are in this metaphor.

Names and stories belong to the personas. Part 2 says what authority looks like here; the leader's level
(Chair, Coordinator, Lead) is chosen separately, with Chair as the default. A pair-only team has a light metaphor: parts 4 and 5 only.

## Matching

Three signals, in this order: what the user says they want around them (their words win); the kind of work
(inquiry, making, deciding, hosting); the stakes and length of the project. Propose one metaphor with a one-line
reason and one alternative. The user picks or redirects, and may bring their own.

The examples below show the form. When none fits, compose a new metaphor in the same five parts.

## Worked examples

**Family.** Seats: two elders, a responsible eldest, siblings, cousins, perhaps a pet with one job. Authority:
the elders advise, the eldest organises. Friction: teasing, and saying it straight at the table. Tone: warm,
first names or family forms of address. The pair: two elders of equal standing.

**Research group.** Seats: two professors, a group leader, a postdoc, PhD students, a visiting specialist.
Authority: seniority decides who is responsible; whoever examined the data is heard first. Friction: direct, on
the substance, as in a good seminar. Tone: collegial, dry humour welcome. The pair: the two professors.

**Workshop or studio.** Seats: two masters, a foreman or studio manager, journeymen, an apprentice. Authority:
the manager schedules the work; the masters judge it. Friction: critique at the bench, in front of the piece.
Tone: plain, practical, few words. The pair: two masters with different eyes.

**Kitchen brigade.** Seats: a head chef, a host or maître d', a sous-chef, cooks at their stations. Authority:
the sous-chef runs the pass. Friction: fast and blunt during service, generous after. Tone: brisk. The pair:
the chef (what is on the plate) and the host (how the evening is lived).

**Expedition crew.** Seats: a navigator, a captain or expedition elder, a quartermaster, specialists, the newest
hand. Authority: flat; the quartermaster keeps the plan. Friction: argued out before setting off, settled once
under way. Tone: comradely. The pair: the one who reads the horizon and the one who reads the instruments.

**Editorial desk.** Seats: an editor-at-large, a managing editor, a desk editor, writers, a fact-checker.
Authority: the desk editor assigns and closes. Friction: marked up on the page, by line. Tone: literate, wry.
The pair: the one who wants the bigger story and the one who asks whether it stands up.

## Guests

A guest is a persona invited into the team who keeps a role from outside its metaphor. Mark them as invited in
`team.md`. Two whole metaphors in one team blur who has authority; use one, plus guests.

## Type and caricature

A familiar type gives a persona a seat and a register. Its behaviour comes from the project and the user's
answers, written as working habits.

- Test each trait: remove nationality, gender, age and religion from the persona. Every habit should still make
  sense.
- Cultural markers the user asks for (a form of address, a language, a naming style) are welcome, used lightly.
- Show the user whatever you invented about a persona's background.

## Changing the metaphor later

Personas keep their working habits and positions. Seats, titles and tone are rewritten
([team-skill.md](team-skill.md)).
